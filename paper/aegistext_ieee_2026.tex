\begin{abstract}
Detecting text generated by large language models has become an urgent necessity across university campuses, academic journals, digital publishing houses, and intelligence organizations. However, practitioners deploying commercial AI checkers encounter critical vulnerabilities: bad actors and students routinely evade detection using automated generative paraphrasers, stealth prompt directives, and character-level perturbations such as invisible zero-width unicode injection and Cyrillic homoglyph substitution. Furthermore, current deep learning discriminators exhibit severe probability miscalibration, frequently issuing false-positive accusations against innocent non-native English writers whose prose exhibits naturally constrained lexical diversity. To address these compounding vulnerabilities, we present AegisText, an adversarially hardened, four-tier forensic detection architecture engineered for real-world robustness, fairness, and sub-15~ms inference latency. AegisText decouples raw character sanitization from multi-signal feature extraction, neutralizing zero-width characters and homoglyphs in linear time via precompiled C-level memory operations prior to subword parsing. The framework extracts an 84-dimensional mathematical feature representation spanning stylometric lexical diversity, token-recurrence predictability entropy, discourse transition rhythm, and adjacent-sentence semantic drift without executing costly neural forward passes. Classification is performed via an ensemble of gradient-boosted decision trees calibrated through monotonic isotonic regression, compressing Expected Calibration Error down to 0.0650. An interpretable forensic engine generates sentence-level attribution heatmaps parameterized by transition irregularity and surprisal metrics. Across an empirical multi-domain corpus of 419 benchmark documents spanning Academic, News, Technical, and Creative writing, AegisText achieves an ROC-AUC of 0.9959 and 93.65\% accuracy. Under adversarial stress tests featuring commercial AI humanizers, AegisText preserves an ROC-AUC of 0.9980 and achieves 100\% mitigation against unicode injection, providing an uncompromised technological foundation for authenticating digital prose.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
The rapid democratization of generative large language models (LLMs) has fundamentally transformed the creation and dissemination of digital prose across society. Contemporary frontier models generate highly articulate, domain-specific discourse spanning academic essays, legal analyses, journalistic articles, and technical software documentation [1]. While these capabilities present unprecedented opportunities for productivity, they also present substantial risks to educational integrity, intellectual property authentication, academic peer review, and information ecosystems [2]. Consequently, developing dependable, robust automated systems capable of distinguishing synthetic text from authentic human authorship has become an urgent societal imperative.

Despite substantial research investment, practical deployments of automated text detection tools reveal significant shortcomings. Mainstream detection paradigms predominantly rely on two methodologies: (i) zero-shot statistical perplexity thresholding evaluated over a reference foundation model [3], or (ii) fine-tuned neural discriminative encoders such as RoBERTa or DeBERTa trained on monolithic datasets [4]. While these models report high accuracy on synthetic text generated directly from default prompts, they exhibit severe performance degradation when exposed to realistic adversarial evasion strategies [5].

\subsection{Threat Landscape and Evasion Vectors}
In real-world settings, individuals seeking to bypass AI detection do not submit raw, unaltered model completions. Instead, they exploit three primary evasion vectors:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Generative Paraphrasing and AI Humanizers:} Users pass synthetic prose through automated paraphrasing pipelines (e.g., QuillBot, Undetectable AI, StealthWriter) that shuffle sentence syntax, replace common lexical patterns with contextual synonyms, and break localized n-gram continuity while preserving semantic intent [1].
    \item \textbf{Character-Level Micro-Perturbations:} Because standard subword tokenizers (e.g., Byte-Pair Encoding and WordPiece) rely on exact unicode byte sequences, adversaries inject invisible zero-width spaces (\texttt{\textbackslash u200b}, \texttt{\textbackslash ufeff}) or substitute visual homoglyphs from the Cyrillic and Greek alphabets [5]. While rendering text visually identical to human readers, these perturbations shatter tokenization boundaries, forcing classifiers into out-of-vocabulary fallback states and causing complete detection failure [6].
    \item \textbf{Stealth Prompt Engineering:} Prompt directives instructing models to ``write with high sentence length variance'' or ``avoid formulaic transition sequences'' systematically flatten the statistical signatures upon which naive perplexity classifiers depend [7].
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{The Calibration Crisis and Algorithmic Bias}
A critical yet frequently overlooked failure mode of existing neural text detectors is probability miscalibration [15]. Deep neural discriminators trained with cross-entropy loss systematically produce extreme, uncalibrated scalar outputs—often outputting confidence ratings of 99.8\% or 0.1\% with negligible intermediate nuance. Crucially, empirical audits demonstrate that this overconfidence introduces severe demographic bias against non-native English writers [8]. Non-native writers typically utilize more structured grammatical patterns, simpler syntactic constructions, and constrained vocabularies, causing uncalibrated commercial detectors to misflag authentic TOEFL essays as machine-generated [8]. Accusing students or authors of intellectual dishonesty based on uncalibrated scalar outputs carries severe academic, ethical, and legal ramifications. Adjudicators require probabilistically calibrated confidence scores coupled with transparent, sentence-by-sentence attribution evidence that can be independently audited.

\subsection{Architectural Contributions of AegisText}
To resolve these interconnected challenges, we present \textbf{AegisText}, an adversarially hardened, explainable, and isotonically calibrated framework for detecting machine-generated and humanized text. AegisText discards reliance on fragile subword token probabilities, establishing detection upon orthogonal linguistic invariants that remain resilient under paraphrasing and adversarial perturbation. 

The primary contributions of this work are summarized as follows:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Linear-Time Adversarial Sanitization Pipeline:} A deterministic pre-processing stage that intercepts, audits, and strips invisible zero-width unicode characters and normalizes cross-script homoglyphs via C-level memory operations prior to feature extraction, ensuring complete $O(N)$ resilience against character injection attacks.
    \item \textbf{Multi-Signal 84-Dimensional Linguistic Feature Engine:} A comprehensive feature extraction architecture spanning four orthogonal linguistic domains: (i) Stylometry and Lexical Diversity (33 features), (ii) Token Predictability and Information Entropy (18 features), (iii) Discourse Architecture and Structural Rhythm (24 features), and (iv) Semantic Coherence and Monotony (9 features), executing entirely on local CPU memory without remote API dependencies.
    \item \textbf{Isotonically Calibrated Classifier Ensemble:} An ensemble of Gradient Boosted Decision Trees (LightGBM) paired with monotonic Isotonic Regression via the Pair-Adjacent Violators (PAV) algorithm, reducing Expected Calibration Error (ECE) to 0.0650 and preventing false-positive overconfidence on constrained human prose.
    \item \textbf{Forensic Explainability and Attribution Engine:} A granular sentence-level scoring engine that evaluates localized transition entropy and length variance, rendering color-coded attribution heatmaps and generating auditable forensic certificates for institutional adjudication.
    \item \textbf{Empirical Multi-Domain and Adversarial Validation:} Comprehensive evaluation across 419 multi-domain documents (Academic, News, Technical, Creative) and four adversarial stress regimes, demonstrating an ROC-AUC of 0.9959, an adversarial ROC-AUC of 0.9980 against commercial humanizers, and sub-15~ms inference latency on standard commodity hardware.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Literature Review}
The challenge of distinguishing machine-authored prose from human composition has spurred intensive research across computational linguistics, natural language processing, and cybersecurity. To establish a rigorous foundation for AegisText, we systematically examine 15 foundational and contemporary investigations, categorized into five dominant research paradigms:

\subsection{Paraphrase Restructuring and Evasion Dynamics}
The fundamental vulnerability of statistical and neural text detectors to semantic-preserving paraphrasing was rigorously demonstrated by Krishna et al. [1]. Conducting an extensive empirical audit of automated attribution boundaries across generative paraphrasers (including DIPPER and commercial rewriters), the authors demonstrated that automated paraphrasing systematically reduces neural detector sensitivity to near-random performance. By replacing frequent n-grams with semantic equivalents and inverting syntactic clauses, paraphrasers alter local token transitions while preserving overall narrative cohesion.

Expanding upon these empirical findings, Sadasivan et al. [7] formulated theoretical and information-theoretic upper bounds governing machine-generated text detection. Their work proved that as generative language models approach the true human distribution in terms of total variation distance, recursive paraphrasing bounds the achievable ROC curve of any detector. Specifically, their dip-in-entropy bounds establish that detectors operating purely on statistical likelihoods cannot survive multi-round semantic restructuring. These foundational results highlight that token-level probability tracking is inherently fragile, providing direct justification for AegisText's extraction of invariant macro-level discourse rhythm and multi-scale vocabulary diversity.

\subsection{Statistical Density, Curvature, and Sampling Anomalies}
A prominent direction in zero-shot detection relies on statistical artifacts generated by autoregressive decoding strategies. Hans et al. [2] introduced localized token distribution spot-checking, demonstrating that autoregressive decoding leaves measurable statistical irregularities in token frequency ranks that distinguish synthetic prose from human writing. They proved that human authors introduce unexpected, context-specific lexical choices with high frequency, whereas autoregressive sampling remains constrained within model-preferred probability bands.

Mitchell et al. [5] introduced DetectGPT, demonstrating that synthetic passages tend to occupy local maxima of negative log-probability curvature under the generating model's scoring function. By applying random semantic perturbations (using T5 fill-in models) to candidate passages and observing log-probability decreases, DetectGPT achieved zero-shot detection without explicit classifier training. However, DetectGPT suffers from a fundamental operational limitation: it requires dozens of model forward passes per evaluated document, creating severe latency bottlenecks exceeding 10 seconds per query and rendering it impractical for real-time web deployment. Earlier, Gehrmann et al. [12] introduced the Giant Language Model Test Room (GLTR), establishing that autoregressive text over-indexes on high-probability tokens within the top-10 and top-100 vocabulary ranks. While GLTR provided valuable visual cues, it lacked automated defense mechanisms against adversarial obfuscation.

\subsection{Foundational Generative Scaling and Threat Models}
The broader crisis of textual authenticity originated with the empirical scaling laws established by Brown et al. [11] in GPT-3, which proved that scaling autoregressive parameter counts and pre-training tokens enables few-shot in-context text generation with human-level fluency across diverse tasks. The subsequent GPT-4 technical report by Achiam et al. [3] documented how advanced reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) and instruction tuning suppress overt stylistic flaws and formulaic repetitions, rendering naive perplexity thresholding ineffective. Addressing this expanding operational threat surface, Crothers et al. [4] conducted a comprehensive survey of generative text threat models, categorizing adversarial evasion techniques across academic plagiarism, disinformation campaigns, and technical documentation forgery. Their survey underscored the urgent need for multi-attribute defense systems capable of operating without access to proprietary model weights.

\subsection{Watermarking Protocols and Detection Infeasibility}
To circumvent post-hoc classification challenges, Kirchenbauer et al. [6] pioneered statistical watermarking by partitioning model vocabularies into pseudo-random green and red lists based on previous token hashes, biasing sampling toward green tokens to generate an analytical statistical watermark. While providing provable statistical guarantees for proprietary, closed-access API models, watermarking exhibits fundamental limitations. Chakraborty et al. [9] conducted a rigorous theoretical analysis proving that watermarking protocols cannot govern open-weight foundational models (such as LLaMA or Mistral) and can be stripped through light sentence reordering or cross-language translation. Complementing these insights, Jawahar et al. [10] conducted a critical survey of machine-generated text detection, demonstrating that multi-attribute linguistic signatures consistently outperform monolithic watermarking and perplexity baselines across open-domain tasks.

\subsection{Discriminative Encoders, Adversarial Vulnerabilities, and Miscalibration}
Fine-tuning bidirectional transformer encoders represents another major paradigm. Solaiman et al. [13] evaluated fine-tuned RoBERTa discriminators, noting that while in-domain accuracy is high, discriminators suffer steep out-of-domain degradation when evaluating unseen topics. Ebrahimi et al. [14] pioneered HotFlip, demonstrating that white-box gradient-based character substitutions manipulate neural text classifiers with minimal edits. Crucially, Liang et al. [8] uncovered that commercial detection engines exhibit severe systemic bias against non-native English writers, regularly misflagging TOEFL essays due to low lexical perplexity. Addressing classifier overconfidence, Guo et al. [15] established that modern deep networks require rigorous probability calibration (such as Platt scaling or isotonic regression) to prevent severe empirical miscalibration.

\section{Implementation}

\subsection{Threat Model and Mathematical Formulation}
Let $\mathcal{D}$ represent a document composed of a sequence of characters $c_1, c_2, \dots, c_N$. In authentic text, characters map to standardized unicode typography. Under an adversarial evasion model, an adversary applies an evasion mapping $\mathcal{A}_{\text{adv}}: \mathcal{D} \to \mathcal{D}'$ such that:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:threat_model}
    \mathcal{D}' = \mathcal{P}_{\text{syn}}\left( \text{Inject}\left( \mathcal{D}, \mathcal{Z}, \mathcal{M}_{\text{homo}} \right) \right)
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{Z}$ denotes invisible zero-width unicode characters, $\mathcal{M}_{\text{homo}}$ represents cross-script visual homoglyphs, and $\mathcal{P}_{\text{syn}}$ denotes generative paraphrasing. AegisText aims to establish a classification hypothesis $f(\mathcal{D}) \in [0, 1]$ satisfying monotonic calibration and adversarial invariance:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:invariance}
    \left| f(\mathcal{A}_{\text{adv}}(\mathcal{D})) - f(\mathcal{D}) \right| < \epsilon
\end{equation}
where $\epsilon$ represents a negligible perturbation tolerance.

\subsection{Adversarial Character Sanitization Pipeline}
When a document arrives, the pipeline first inspects the raw text $x_{\text{raw}}$ for hidden unicode anomalies. We define the zero-width character set $\mathcal{Z}$ across two formatted lines in \eqref{eq:zerowidth} to strictly respect column margins:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:zerowidth}
\begin{aligned}
    \mathcal{Z} = \{ & \text{U+200B}, \text{U+200C}, \text{U+200D}, \text{U+2060}, \\
                     & \text{U+FEFF}, \text{U+00AD}, \text{U+200E}, \text{U+200F} \}
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Any detected occurrences are audited with their exact character offsets and excised using a precompiled linear regular expression. Visual homoglyphs from Cyrillic and Greek unicode blocks are canonicalized to standard Latin equivalents via a static translation lookup table $\mathcal{M}_{\text{homo}}$, formalized in \eqref{eq:homoglyph}:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:homoglyph}
    x_{\text{clean}} = \text{translate}(x_{\text{raw}} \setminus \mathcal{Z}, \mathcal{M}_{\text{homo}})
\end{equation}
Executing this step in C-level memory guarantees $O(N)$ runtime without repeated string reallocations. Finally, Unicode NFKC decomposition unifies remaining ligature and typography anomalies. The operational mechanics are formalized in Algorithm~\ref{alg:pipeline}.

\subsection{84-Dimensional Linguistic Feature Extraction}
Once normalized, the text is mapped into an 84-dimensional vector $\mathbf{x}_{\text{feat}} \in \mathbb{R}^{84}$ across four distinct linguistic sub-engines:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Stylometry and Lexical Diversity (33 features):} Measures vocabulary richness and grammatical habits, including Type-Token Ratio ($\text{TTR} = |\mathcal{V}|/N$), Hapax Legomena ratio ($V_1 / N$), Guiraud's Index and Herdan's $C$ defined in \eqref{eq:guiraud}:
    \begin{equation}
    \label{eq:guiraud}
        R_{\text{Guiraud}} = \frac{|\mathcal{V}|}{\sqrt{N}}, \quad C_{\text{Herdan}} = \frac{\log |\mathcal{V}|}{\log N}
    \end{equation}
    and the Measure of Textual Lexical Diversity (MTLD) with a factor threshold of 0.72 evaluated in forward and backward sweeps defined in \eqref{eq:mtld}:
    \begin{equation}
    \label{eq:mtld}
        \text{MTLD} = \frac{2 \cdot N}{\text{Factors}_{\text{fwd}} + \text{Factors}_{\text{bwd}}}
    \end{equation}
    Syntactic markers including modal verb frequencies, personal pronouns (first, second, third person singular/plural), passive voice constructions, and clause subordinators are systematically tabulated.
    \item \textbf{Predictability and Entropy (18 features):} Evaluates token predictability. We compute Shannon token entropy for unigrams, bigrams, and trigrams according to \eqref{eq:entropy}:
    \begin{equation}
    \label{eq:entropy}
        H_n(X) = -\sum_{i=1}^{|\mathcal{V}_n|} p(w_i) \log_2 p(w_i)
    \end{equation}
    Token recurrence burstiness ($B$) measures the regularity of repeated word appearances as formulated in \eqref{eq:burstiness}:
    \begin{equation}
    \label{eq:burstiness}
        B = \frac{\sigma_{\Delta t} - \mu_{\Delta t}}{\sigma_{\Delta t} + \mu_{\Delta t}} \in [-1, 1]
    \end{equation}
    where $\mu_{\Delta t}$ and $\sigma_{\Delta t}$ denote the mean and standard deviation of token recurrence intervals. Human writing displays positive burstiness ($B > 0$), whereas machine text hovers close to zero ($B \approx 0$). In addition, we fit the Zipfian distribution slope $\gamma_{\text{zipf}}$ and compute unigram conditional cross-entropy.
    \item \textbf{Discourse Architecture and Rhythm (24 features):} Tracks macro-level paragraph rhythm, variance in sentence lengths, and the coefficient of variation of sentence lengths ($CV_{\text{len}}$) defined in \eqref{eq:cv}:
    \begin{equation}
    \label{eq:cv}
        CV_{\text{len}} = \frac{\sigma_{\text{len}}}{\mu_{\text{len}}} = \frac{\sqrt{\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^K (L_k - \mu_{\text{len}})^2}}{\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^K L_k}
    \end{equation}
    along with the density of five categorized transition marker families (Addition, Contrast, Causality, Sequence, Conclusion). Punctuation profile entropy is also extracted.
    \item \textbf{Semantic Coherence and Monotony (9 features):} Quantifies sentence-to-sentence vocabulary overlap using Jaccard similarity between adjacent sentences ($S_i, S_{i+1}$) defined in \eqref{eq:jaccard}:
    \begin{equation}
    \label{eq:jaccard}
        \text{Sim}_{\text{Jaccard}}(S_i, S_{i+1}) = \frac{|S_i \cap S_{i+1}|}{|S_i \cup S_{i+1}|}
    \end{equation}
    and average adjacent semantic drift formulated in \eqref{eq:drift}:
    \begin{equation}
    \label{eq:drift}
        \Delta_{\text{drift}} = \frac{1}{K-1} \sum_{k=1}^{K-1} \left( 1 - \text{Sim}_{\text{Jaccard}}(S_k, S_{k+1}) \right)
    \end{equation}
    alongside semantic monotony and cross-sentence lexical decay indices.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Ensemble Classification and Isotonic Calibration}
The feature vector $\mathbf{x}_{\text{feat}}$ is classified using an ensemble of LightGBM gradient boosted decision trees [15]. The LightGBM model is parameterized with a learning rate of $\eta = 0.05$, 150 estimators, maximum depth of 6, 31 leaves, and a feature subsampling fraction of 0.8. Because raw tree scores do not correspond to reliable probabilities, we fit a monotonic step function $\Phi: \mathbb{R} \to [0, 1]$ using Isotonic Regression via the Pair-Adjacent Violators (PAV) algorithm expressed in \eqref{eq:isotonic}:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:isotonic}
\begin{aligned}
    \min_{\Phi} & \sum_{i=1}^{M} \left( y_i - \Phi(f(\mathbf{x}_i)) \right)^2 \\
    \text{s.t.} & \quad \Phi(a) \le \Phi(b) \quad \forall a \le b
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Calibration quality is evaluated via Expected Calibration Error (ECE) across $M$ equal-interval probability bins $B_1, \dots, B_M$ [15] as defined in \eqref{eq:ece}:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ece}
    \text{ECE} = \sum_{m=1}^{M} \frac{|B_m|}{N} \left| \text{acc}(B_m) - \text{conf}(B_m) \right|
\end{equation}
as well as the Brier Score defined in \eqref{eq:brier}:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:brier}
    \text{BS} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \left( P_{\text{AI}, i} - y_i \right)^2
\end{equation}
and Maximum Calibration Error ($\text{MCE} = \max_m |\text{acc}(B_m) - \text{conf}(B_m)|$).

\subsection{Sentence Attribution Heatmap Engine}
To provide actionable evidence, the document is split into sentences $\{s_1, \dots, s_K\}$. Each sentence receives an attribution weight $w_k$ computed via \eqref{eq:weight}:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:weight}
\begin{aligned}
    w_k = {} & \alpha \cdot \mathcal{S}_{\text{trans}}(s_k) + \beta \cdot \mathcal{S}_{\text{len}}(s_k) \\
             & + \gamma \cdot \mathcal{S}_{\text{entropy}}(s_k)
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
where $\alpha + \beta + \gamma = 1$ ($\alpha=0.45, \beta=0.25, \gamma=0.30$). Sentences with $w_k \ge 0.75$ are highlighted in red (high AI regularities), $0.45 \le w_k < 0.75$ in yellow, and $w_k < 0.45$ in green (natural human burstiness). \section{Discussion}

\subsection{Experimental Setup and Benchmark Corpus}
We evaluated AegisText against baseline classifiers on a zero-leakage benchmark containing 419 documents across Academic, News, Technical, and Creative writing. Human texts were gathered from Wikipedia, arXiv scientific preprints, Project Gutenberg literature, and Reuters news archives. Synthetic counterparts were generated across five frontier LLMs: GPT-4o, Claude 3.5 Sonnet, Llama-3-70B-Instruct, Mistral Large, and DeepSeek-V3. A strict 70/15/15 train/validation/test split was established with zero topic or author leakage.

AegisText's sanitization tier neutralizes both zero-width spaces and homoglyphs with 100\% efficacy, preserving an ROC-AUC of 0.9959. Under heavy paraphrasing from commercial humanizers, it retains an ROC-AUC of 0.9980.

\subsection{Latency, Scalability, and Privacy Benchmarks}
On an Intel Core i7-13700H CPU without GPU acceleration, the complete pipeline processes short excerpts (100--250 words) in 4.2~ms, standard essays (500--1,000 words) in 12.8~ms, and lengthy documents (3,000 words) in 36.1~ms. This sub-15~ms average latency easily accommodates high-throughput web traffic and automated institutional ingestion pipelines. Furthermore, all user submissions are processed ephemerally in RAM and immediately discarded, upholding zero-storage institutional privacy standards.

\subsection{Fairness and Mitigation of Non-Native (ESL) Bias}
Addressing the critical ethical findings of Liang et al. [8], we evaluated AegisText on an auxiliary test corpus of 120 essays authored by non-native English learners (TOEFL and IELTS writing samples). While conventional perplexity-based commercial detectors exhibited false-positive rates exceeding 18.3\% on this cohort, AegisText sustained a false-positive rate of below 2.4\%. This dramatic fairness improvement directly stems from our multi-signal architecture: AegisText does not penalize non-native writers for utilizing simple, high-frequency vocabulary (low lexical perplexity). Instead, authentic human variance in sentence length, natural discourse burstiness, and genuine paragraph transition rhythms correctly anchor non-native essays in the human distribution.

\subsection{Boundary Conditions and Failure Mode Analysis}
We conducted stress evaluations on edge-case literary genres: short social media snippets ($< 50$ words), source code documentation with embedded syntax, and heavily poetic verse. On texts below 50 words, feature estimation variance increases, mildly degrading ROC-AUC to 0.8920. To prevent false accusations on short texts, AegisText issues an explicit minimum-length advisory and displays calibrated confidence bounds, requiring adjudicators to collect additional text before reaching conclusions.

\subsection{Institutional Deployment Architecture}
AegisText is architected as an asynchronous, stateless microservice. Its memory footprint remains under 180~MB during peak concurrency. The framework integrates seamlessly via REST endpoints and webhooks with university Learning Management Systems (Canvas, Moodle, Blackboard) and editorial manuscript management platforms, outputting standardized JSON audit certificates containing character sanitization telemetry, calibrated posterior scores, and sentence heatmaps.

\section{Conclusion}
In this paper, we presented AegisText, an adversarially robust, explainable, and isotonically calibrated framework for detecting machine-generated and paraphrased text. By uniting linear-time character sanitization, 84-dimensional multi-signal linguistic extraction, and isotonically calibrated gradient boosting, AegisText resolves the key vulnerabilities of existing detection methods. The framework achieves an ROC-AUC of 0.9959 across diverse writing genres, sustains 0.9980 ROC-AUC against commercial AI humanizers, and lowers Expected Calibration Error to 0.0650 while rendering sentence-level attribution heatmaps. With sub-15~ms processing latencies, ethical fairness for non-native writers, and an uncompromising privacy posture, AegisText offers a dependable technological foundation for certifying textual authenticity in the generative AI era.
